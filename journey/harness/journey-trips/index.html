A journey through my research

It starts with the planet under our feet. Scroll to look inside it, then ride along with two space missions, and go beyond, wherever the same physics is at work.

A wobbling spin

The Earth's axis slowly sweeps out a cone, once every 26,000 years: that is precession. The pull of the Moon and the Sun adds smaller nods on top, the nutations, the largest with an 18.6-year period.

Sped up enormously here, with the nods exaggerated about a thousand times.

Inside

Cut through the poles, the Earth shows its layers: a rocky mantle, a liquid iron outer core and a solid inner core. The liquid core does not simply follow the mantle as it nods.

Waves in the liquid core

Each nod forces the core at nearly one cycle per day. In a rotating fluid this launches inertial waves, which gather into thin shear layers inclined at 60° to the equator, springing from 30° latitude on the core's boundaries. The friction in these layers affects how the Earth nutates.

A magnetic planet

The churning of liquid iron in the core sustains the Earth's magnetic field. The field acts back on the flow in turn: it shapes the waves travelling through the core, and electric currents near the core's boundary couple it to the mantle, leaving their mark on the length of the day and on the nutations.

Drawn here as a simple dipole along the spin axis.

Flows at the top of the core

Just below the core's surface the liquid iron is on the move: large gyres and smaller eddies, mostly drifting westward, with waves running along the equator. The field lines are anchored in this moving metal and get dragged along, swaying and twisting as the flow shifts. Watching the field change is how we map the flow.

Colours show the flow: amber eastward, teal westward. An illustration, not a model, and greatly sped up.

Space missions · the same questions, asked from orbit

Off to Mercury

In October 2018 an Ariane 5 launched BepiColombo, the mission of the European and Japanese space agencies to Mercury. That bright dot leaving the Earth is our ride.

Braking all the way

Falling towards the Sun speeds a spacecraft up, so reaching Mercury means braking all the way: eight years of electric propulsion and nine flybys, of the Earth once, Venus twice and Mercury six times. Near the end, the transfer module that carried the two orbiters lets go.

A sketch of a path, not the real one (18 laps around the Sun). The spacecraft is a simplified model, drawn 30 times larger (it is 30 m across its wings).

Into orbit

On 21 November 2026 the two orbiters, ESA's MPO and JAXA's Mio, still stacked together, are due to be captured into a polar orbit around Mercury. In December they part ways: Mio stays on a wide orbit to study the magnetic field and its surroundings, while MPO descends closer to map the planet. Science begins in spring 2027.

Mercury: same physics, another planet

The same questions apply to Mercury, with telling differences. Its liquid core fills over 80% of the planet's radius, against 55% for the Earth. It librates rather than nutates: its spin speeds up and slows down every 88 days, launching shear layers at 70° to the equator. And its field is a hundred times weaker than the Earth's, centred some 480 km north of the planet's centre.

The rocking is exaggerated (really about 40 arcseconds). The darker band is a possible stable layer at the top of the core.

Meanwhile, on the way to Jupiter

Another mission is heading further out: ESA's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer, JUICE, launched in April 2023. It too takes the long way round, borrowing speed from the Earth and the Moon, Venus, and the Earth again, to reach Jupiter in July 2031.

Jupiter's moons, in step

For every orbit of Ganymede, Europa goes round twice and Io four times. These regular tugs keep their orbits slightly eccentric, so Jupiter's tides never stop flexing them.

Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. Europa and Ganymede, and perhaps Callisto, hide oceans beneath their ice: ocean worlds, which we will meet again at Saturn.

Arriving in July 2031, JUICE spends three and a half years looping through the system, with some 35 flybys, before settling around Ganymede.

Down to Ganymede

At the end of 2034 JUICE will brake into orbit around Ganymede, the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than our own, then spiral down to just 500 km above the surface.

Measuring how Ganymede's shape, gravity and rotation respond to Jupiter's pull will help reveal the ocean inside.

The spacecraft is drawn 30 times larger. Ganymede and Jupiter are to scale.

Mapping Ganymede: surface and tides

Down at 500 km, on a polar orbit, JUICE goes round Ganymede about every three hours, and Ganymede turns slowly beneath it, once a week. Each pass sweeps a strip from pole to pole, some 6° further west each time, and the moon comes into focus strip by strip.

The laser altimeter also catches the surface rising and falling as Ganymede's slightly eccentric orbit varies Jupiter's pull: by about seven metres if an ocean lies beneath the ice, by well under one if not.

Scroll to fly the passes. The strips are drawn far wider than the instruments' real footprints, and the tide thousands of times larger than it is.

Gravity

Each pass also bends JUICE's path, ever so slightly, where Ganymede's gravity is stronger or weaker. Tracked from Earth by its radio signal, to a fraction of a millimetre per second, those nudges add up to a map of the gravity field: how the interior is layered, and, from how the field changes with the tides, how deep the ocean lies.

Colours: gravity higher (red) or lower (blue) than average. Only the two largest terms are measured (by Galileo, from its flybys); the rest is illustrative.

Magnetic field

Ganymede is the only moon known to make its own magnetic field, in a liquid iron core. On top of it, Jupiter's tilted field sweeps past every ten and a half hours, and a salty ocean answers with a field of its own (in teal), swelling and fading in step.

JUICE's magnetometer will measure that answer, and with it how deep and how salty the ocean is.

Both fields drawn as simple dipoles; the ocean's is far weaker than drawn.

Three ocean worlds

Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, side by side at their true sizes. All three are thought to hide a salty ocean of liquid water beneath their ice, and each is a different kind of ocean world.

My work asks the same questions of all three: how deep their oceans lie, how the water moves, how they answer Jupiter's tides, and what signatures they leave that a spacecraft can measure.

How deep?

Europa's ocean may lie only a few tens of kilometres down, about 100 km deep, in direct contact with its rocky floor. Ganymede's sits some 150 km below the surface, sandwiched between ice above and layers of high-pressure ice below. Callisto's, if it exists, is thinner and deeper still, over an interior where ice and rock never fully separated.

Those differences decide whether the water touches rock, how heat and salts move through it, and how strongly each moon flexes under Jupiter's pull.

Interiors drawn to scale from representative models; the thicknesses are uncertain, often by a factor of two.

Beyond · wherever a fluid spins

Enceladus

A small icy moon of Saturn, barely 500 km across. Jets of water vapour and ice burst from fractures near its south pole, fed by an ocean hidden beneath the ice and by a source of heat that is still debated.

An ocean that rings

Beneath about 20 km of ice lies a global ocean some 40 km deep. Enceladus always keeps the same face to Saturn, but its slightly eccentric orbit flexes the moon and rocks its shell back and forth once per orbit.

In the rotating ocean, this forcing launches inertial waves: the same shear layers as in Earth's core, squeezed into a thin shell.

Layers to scale; the ocean's depth is uncertain.

In tune

The ocean has its own natural modes of oscillation. When one of them is close to the tidal frequency, the waves resonate, and the friction that turns their motion into heat rises sharply.

How much heat such resonances can supply is one of the questions behind my work on ocean worlds.

The Sun

Its outer third churns with convection, and its rotation supports slow, swirling inertial waves. With colleagues, I helped identify such modes in the solar convection zone.

July 2031 · Arrival

JUICE swings past Ganymede, then fires its main engine to brake into orbit around Jupiter: a first loop reaching out more than 250 times Jupiter's radius.

Early 2032 · Shrinking the loops

Flyby after flyby of Ganymede bends the orbit tighter, from months per loop down to a couple of weeks.

July 2032 · Europa

Two close passes of Europa, on 2 and 17 July: the mission's only look at its icy crust up close.

2033 · Up high

Callisto's pull tilts the orbit up to 35° out of Jupiter's equator, to look down on the poles and the magnetosphere.

2034 · Slowing down

Back near the equator, more passes of Callisto and Ganymede bleed off speed until JUICE drifts alongside Ganymede.

October 2034 · Caught

Ganymede captures JUICE. What follows is the spiral down to its final orbit: back to the journey.
